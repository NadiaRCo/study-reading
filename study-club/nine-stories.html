Story 1 of 9 · Stage 1

KW Chapter 1 · The scientific study of politics

The Question by the Fire

Before counting anything, turn a wise saying into a question that evidence can answer.

The story

Picture an evening by the fire. An elder says: “When the harvest is good, people praise the chief. When the harvest fails, they grumble and look for a new one.”

Everyone nods. It sounds true. But a curious young listener asks: “Is it always true? How would we know? What if the chief was lucky, or people liked his family anyway?”

That young listener is doing political science. This whole course is about how to take a claim that sounds true and check it carefully, honestly, and with evidence.

Frame 1.1

Politics can be studied scientifically

Science isn't lab coats. It's a way of asking: make a clear claim, decide what evidence would support it or show it's wrong, look at the evidence, and change your mind if the evidence says so.

The elder's saying has a modern version, and it's the example you will meet again and again in this course, all the way to Chapter 9:

When the economy is doing well, does the party already in power (the incumbent) win more votes?

Pause and think: what would make the elder's saying “scientific”?

Saying exactly what is claimed (good harvests → more support for the chief), and being willing to check it against real cases, including cases that might prove it wrong.

Frame 1.2

The road to knowledge

Question
Something puzzling about politics. “Why do some leaders lose power?”
Theory
A possible explanation of why. “Voters judge leaders by how well people are living.”
Hypothesis
What we'd expect to see if the theory is right. “When the economy grows faster, the incumbent party gets more votes.”
Null hypothesis
What we'd see if the theory is wrong: no relationship. “Growth makes no difference to the vote.”
Measure → test → evaluate
Turn the ideas into numbers, compare with evidence, and decide how much more (or less) confident we are.
Provisional knowledge
Our best answer for now. New evidence can change it.
Pause and think: what's the difference between a theory and a hypothesis?

A theory is the explanation (why). A hypothesis is a specific thing you'd expect to observe if the explanation were true (what you'd see).

Frame 1.3

X causes Y: the two main characters

Almost every question in this course has two main characters:

X: the cause

The independent variable. It's what we think makes a difference. Here: economic growth.

Y: the outcome

The dependent variable. It's what we're trying to explain. Here: the incumbent party's vote share.

A variable is anything that varies: it takes different values in different cases. Growth is high in some years and low in others. If something never changes, it can't explain anything that does.

Pause and think: in “more education leads to higher income”, which is X and which is Y?

X = education (the cause). Y = income (the outcome we're explaining).

Frame 1.4

KW's rules of the road

  1. Focus on causes. We want to know if X causes Y, not only whether they happen together.
  2. Don't let the data alone write the theory. Think first about why, then look.
  3. Rely on evidence, not on how strongly people believe something.
  4. Keep “is” separate from “should”. “Growth helps incumbents” is an empirical claim (checkable). “Leaders should be punished for bad economies” is a normative claim (a value judgment).
  5. Aim for general and simple (parsimony): an explanation that covers many cases with few parts.
Pause and think: empirical or normative? “Women should hold half the seats in parliament.”

Normative: it says what should be. The empirical version would be: “Countries with gender quotas have more women in parliament.”

Frame 1.5

“Usually”, not “always”

Social science claims are probabilistic. “Good economies help incumbents” means they tend to do better, not that every incumbent with a good economy wins.

Like the weather: “cloudy skies bring rain” is useful even though some cloudy days stay dry. One dry cloudy day doesn't destroy the idea.

That's also why we need many cases and statistics: one example can't settle a “usually” claim.

In class

  • The chain: question → causal theory → hypothesis → variables and measures → empirical test → evaluation → provisional knowledge.
  • Social science is hard because labs are rare, key ideas (democracy, conflict, rights) are argued over, and relationships are probabilistic.
  • A concept is different from its measure: how we define and measure “economic performance” can change what we find. (Story 5 is all about this.)
  • For any empirical question ask: What is Y? What varies? Compared with what? What evidence would support or challenge the claim?

The Great Voting Question · part 1

Question: does a good economy keep the governing party in power? Theory: voters reward good times and punish bad times. Hypothesis: more growth → more votes for the incumbent party. Null: no relationship. Cases: 36 US presidential elections, 1876 to 2016. Each election is one case. Keep this question in mind: by Story 9 we'll measure the answer with a line.

Words to keep

Theory
An explanation of why X affects Y.
Hypothesis
An observable expectation that follows from the theory.
Null hypothesis
The “nothing is going on” expectation.
Independent variable (X)
The cause.
Dependent variable (Y)
The outcome.
Empirical vs normative
What is (checkable) vs what should be (values).
Probabilistic
Changes the chances, not every single case.
DrumbeatA theory says why; a hypothesis says what we'd see; the evidence decides how confident we can be, for now.

Story 2 of 9 · Stage 1

KW Chapter 2 · The art of theory building

The Storyteller's Craft

A good theory, like a good story, explains why things happen, and can travel to other places.

The story

A weak storyteller lists characters: “There was a king, a drought, and an angry crowd.” A great storyteller explains why: “The drought emptied the granaries, the king kept collecting taxes anyway, and the hungry crowd decided he had broken his promise to protect them.”

The second version has a mechanism: a chain of reasons that connects the drought to the anger. It could also be told about a different king in a different land and still make sense.

That's what theory building is: not a recipe, but disciplined creativity.

Frame 2.1

A variable is not a theory

Saying “education matters” or “institutions matter” is only naming a character. A theory must say how X leads to Y, what has to be true for that to happen, and what we'd observe if it's right.

Pause and think: turn “the economy matters for elections” into a theory.

“Voters look at whether their lives got better under the current government. When times are good they give the government credit and vote to keep it; when times are bad they blame it and vote for the other side.” That's a mechanism: judgment of performance → reward or punishment.

Frame 2.2

Start with interesting variation in Y

Good theories often start with noticing that Y differs. Why do some governments fall while others survive? KW names two kinds of variation:

Cross-sectional

Compare many units at the same time. Many voters in the 2016 election; many countries in 2020.

Time-series

Follow one unit over time. US presidential elections from 1876 to 2016; one country's protests year by year.

Changing the kind of variation changes the question. “Why did this voter choose the incumbent?” (cross-sectional) is different from “Why does the incumbent party do better in some years?” (time-series).

Pause and think: the 36 elections from 1876 to 2016. Which kind of variation?

Time-series: one country (the US) followed across many elections.

Frame 2.3

Drop the proper nouns

Try retelling your explanation without names of people, countries or dates. If “Rwanda” or “Obama” must be in it, it may only describe one case. If the logic still works without the names, it can travel and be tested elsewhere.

“Obama won in 2012 because the economy recovered” → “Incumbents win when the economy improves before the election.” The second sentence can be tested in many elections.

Frame 2.4

Three ways to be original

  1. A new Y: explain an outcome nobody has explained well yet.
  2. A new X: offer a new cause for a familiar outcome.
  3. A new Z: show a third factor that changes the X→Y story: when, where, or for whom X matters, or whether the original link was misleading.
Class example: education, ability, earnings

“More education → higher earnings” looks clear. But add Z = ability: more able people may both stay in school longer and earn more. Adding Z can change the causal story. You'll see this idea again in Story 3 (confounders).

Frame 2.5

Theories as simple models: the turnout puzzle

Some theories are written as small formulas about choices. The famous one is about why anyone bothers to vote (Riker and Ordeshook):

R = P × B − C (+ D)
R
The reward from voting.
P
The probability that your one vote decides the election (tiny in a big election).
B
The benefit if your side wins.
C
The cost of voting (time, travel, effort).
D
Added later: the satisfaction of doing your civic duty, showing who you are, social pressure.

Without D, the formula predicts that almost nobody votes (P is tiny, so P × B is smaller than C). But millions vote. That puzzle made researchers ask what else motivates people. The lesson: when a theory fails, don't just stick a label on the gap. Turn the gap into new, testable explanations (duty, identity, pressure, habit).

Pause and think: why is adding D risky if D just means “whatever makes people vote”?

Then the theory can explain anything and can never be proven wrong. D only helps if you spell out what it is (duty, social pressure…) and test each part.

Frame 2.6

Rules matter: institutions

Outcomes depend on people's preferences and on the rules that combine them. Three voters can each have sensible rankings, yet majority voting between pairs can go round in a circle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A). Then whoever sets the agenda (the order of votes) can decide the winner. Institutions aren't background noise; they can be the mechanism.

The Great Voting Question · part 2

Mechanism: voters look back at performance and reward or punish (retrospective voting). Variation: time-series (elections over 140 years). A rival story, a new Z: a famous 1960 study, The American Voter, argued most people vote from party loyalty learned in their families, so campaigns and short-term events change few votes. Two characters now compete to explain Y: performance and loyalty.

Words to keep

Mechanism
The chain of reasons linking X to Y.
Cross-sectional
Many units, one time.
Time-series
One unit, many times.
Drop the proper nouns
A test of whether a theory can travel.
Z
A third variable that can change the X→Y story.
Parsimony
Explaining a lot with a little.
DrumbeatNaming a variable isn't a theory; explaining how X moves Y, in a way that travels, is.

Exam move

A

Explain what makes a good theory: a mechanism, interesting variation in Y, generality (drop the proper nouns), and the three routes to originality (new Y, new X, new Z).

B

Apply it: “Economic voting has a mechanism (reward/punish), uses time-series variation across elections, and party loyalty is a possible new Z that changes how much the economy matters.”

Story 3 of 9 · Stage 1

KW Chapter 3 · Evaluating causal relationships

The Four Gates

Two things happening together is only one gate. A causal story must pass all four.

The story

Every morning the rooster crows, and then the sun rises. Every single morning. Does the rooster make the sun rise?

Of course not. But notice what the rooster story does have: the two things happen together, perfectly, every day. What it lacks is a believable reason, and the timing fools us: the rooster crows because it senses dawn coming.

This story is about the difference between “happens together” and “causes”, and the four gates a claim must pass.

Frame 3.1

What “X causes Y” really means

A causal claim says: if we changed X, Y would change (its chances or its typical level). That's stronger than “X and Y go together”.

The honest comparison would be the same case with X and without X, at the same moment. But we only ever see one version of the world. The missing version is called the counterfactual.

Did the medicine cure your headache? You'd need to see the same you, same day, without the medicine. You can't. So we look for comparisons that come as close as possible.

Frame 3.2

The four gates

  1. Mechanism: is there a believable way X could produce Y? (The rooster fails here.)
  2. Direction: can we rule out Y causing X?
  3. Covariation: do X and Y actually move together in the data?
  4. Confounding: is there a Z that causes both, making the X–Y link misleading?

Score each gate yes / no / maybe. [y y y y] is the strongest claim. The gates aren't equal: no mechanism or a serious confounder does the most damage.

Frame 3.3

Gate 4, the sneaky one: confounders

People who go to hospital are often less healthy afterwards than people who don't. Do hospitals make people sick? No: how sick you were (Z) sends you to hospital and affects your later health.

Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together. Ice cream doesn't drown anyone. Hot summer weather (Z) causes both.

Pause and think: richer countries are more democratic. Name one possible confounder.

Education is one: more educated populations may both earn more and demand democracy. Others: history of colonial rule, natural resources, geography. A Z must plausibly affect both X and Y.

Frame 3.4

Gate 2: which way does the arrow point?

Are happier citizens the reason democracies are stable, or do stable democracies make citizens happier? Life satisfaction and democratic stability could push each other. That's a reverse causation worry.

Timing helps: if X happens before Y, Y can't have caused X. That's why researchers like data collected over time.

Frame 3.5

Two reminders from class

  • An association is not automatically a clean causal effect. Race and political participation are related, but that pattern alone doesn't tell us what causes what; history, resources, mobilization and barriers to voting are all part of the story.
  • Measurement matters too. Even a claim that passes all four gates can fall apart if “democracy” or “corruption” was measured badly (Story 5). And timing matters: a judicial or institutional factor might change how democracy and corruption appear related.

The Great Voting Question · part 3

Scorecard for growth → incumbent vote: Mechanism yes (reward/punish). Direction mostly yes: the economy is measured in the year before the vote, so the vote can't have caused it, though expected winners might affect confidence a little. Covariation: we'll check in Story 8 (r = .57). Confounding: maybe. A war, a scandal, or party loyalty could affect both the economy and support. So: [y y ? ?] for now.

Words to keep

Causal claim
Changing X would change Y.
Counterfactual
What would have happened without X: the version of the world we never see.
Covariation
X and Y move together.
Reverse causation
Y causes X, not the other way.
Confounder (Z)
A third variable causing both X and Y.
Spurious
A relationship that disappears once Z is accounted for.
DrumbeatFour gates: a reason, the right direction, moving together, and no hidden Z behind both.

Exam move

A

Explain the four hurdles and the counterfactual, and say which failures hurt most (no mechanism, a serious confounder).

B

Score a claim: name X, Y and a possible Z; go gate by gate (yes/no/maybe); say what evidence or comparison would make it more convincing.

Story 4 of 9 · Stage 2

KW Chapter 4 · Research design

The Coin That Decides

Let a coin decide who gets the treatment, and the two groups become fair to compare.

The story

A school starts a free tutoring club. At the end of term, club members have higher grades. Success?

Wait. Who joined the club? The keen students, the ones whose parents push them, the ones who were already trying hard. They might have done better anyway.

Now imagine the head teacher flips a coin for each student: heads, you're in the club; tails, you're not. Keen and less keen students end up in both groups in about equal numbers. If the club group still does better, the club is the most believable reason.

Frame 4.1

Compared with what?

We can never see the same student with and without the club (the missing counterfactual from Story 3). So every design is a way of building a fair comparison group. Always ask: compared with what?

Comparing “people who took X” with “people who didn't” is risky because of selection: people who choose X are often different to start with.

Frame 4.2

The experiment: random assignment

In an experiment, the researcher decides who gets X, by chance. Because a coin can't know who is keen, rich or clever, the groups end up balanced on average on everything, even things nobody measured. That knocks out gate 4 (confounders).

Random assignment

Who gets the treatment. Gives internal validity: confidence the effect is causal.

Random sampling

Who gets into the study. Gives external validity: confidence results apply to the population (Story 7).

Pause and think: a national survey picks 1,000 people at random and asks if they watched a debate. Is this an experiment?

No. It has random sampling (good for generalising) but people chose whether to watch the debate. Nobody assigned the debate by chance, so it isn't an experiment.

Frame 4.3

Three kinds of experiments

Lab experiment
In a controlled room. Tight control, but an artificial setting.
Survey experiment
Randomly give different respondents different question wordings or stories. Large samples, but answers are about hypothetical situations.
Field experiment
In the real world (e.g. randomly chosen neighbourhoods get a program). Most realistic, but costly, less control, and with ethical limits.

Many big causes (war, poverty, dictatorship) can't ethically be randomised at all. So researchers look for other designs.

Frame 4.4

When you can't flip the coin

Observational study + controls
Observe X as it happens, then compare people with the same value of Z. Weakness: you can only control for the Zs you measured.
Natural experiment
Nature or a policy flips the coin for you: the Vietnam-era US draft lottery picked birthdays at random.
Regression discontinuity
Compare people just above and just below a cutoff. In Chile, students just over a test-score line could get college loans; students just under couldn't. They're almost identical except for the loan.
Instrumental variable
Find something (Z) that changes X but affects Y only through X. Example: rainfall → economic growth → civil conflict. Critics ask: could rain affect conflict directly (floods, food prices)?

In class: the prof's examples

ExampleDesignThe worry
Microcredit (Hyderabad)Field experiment: lender expansion randomised by neighbourhoodA naive borrower vs non-borrower comparison mixes the loan with ambition (selection). Result: more borrowing, small average effects.

The Great Voting Question · part 4

Nobody can randomly assign a booming or shrinking economy to an election, so economic voting is studied with an observational design: compare elections across time. That leaves gate 4 open. The loyalty camp used random-sample surveys and re-interviewed the same people in 1956, 1958 and 1960 (a panel). Much later, field experiments randomly assigned campaign contact: door-knocking raised turnout (Gerber & Green, 2000), and 49 experiments found campaign persuasion barely changes who people vote for (Kalla & Broockman, 2018).

Words to keep

Research design
The comparison you set up to answer a causal question.
Selection
People who choose X differ from those who don't.
Random assignment
Chance decides who gets X → fair groups.
Internal validity
Is the effect really causal here?
External validity
Does it apply beyond this study?
Natural experiment
Chance assignment created by nature or policy.
DrumbeatRandom assignment lets me claim cause; random sampling lets me generalise.

Story 5 of 9 · Stage 2

KW Chapter 5 · Measuring concepts of interest

Naming the Thing

A number only helps if it measures the idea you actually meant.

The story

Your aunt says, “She's a good neighbour.” What does she mean? Someone who greets you? Lends you sugar? Keeps quiet at night? Shows up when someone is sick?

If you wanted to measure “good neighbour”, you'd have to decide which of these count, and how to score them. Choose only “greets you” and you'd miss what matters most.

Political science has the same problem with big ideas like democracy, tolerance, or human rights. Before counting, we have to name the thing carefully.

Frame 5.1

From idea to number

Concept → dimensions → variable → indicator.

Concept
The big idea: democracy.
Dimensions
Its parts: real competition (contestation) and who gets to take part (inclusiveness), following Robert Dahl.
Variable / indicator
The actual number: e.g. a Polity score from −10 (very autocratic) to +10 (very democratic).

One indicator usually captures only part of a concept. That's why two studies using different measures of “democracy” can disagree.

Pause and think: what might a Polity score miss?

Inclusiveness: a country can score high on competitive elections while many people can't vote. It also relies on experts' coding judgments.

Frame 5.2

Reliable? Valid? Two different questions

Reliable
Consistent: measure again, get the same result. (Dots close together.)
Valid
Measures the right thing. (Dots on the bullseye.)

A bathroom scale that always shows 2 kg too much is perfectly reliable (same answer every time) but not valid (it's wrong every time).

Frame 5.3

Two kinds of error

Systematic bias

Pushes every measurement the same way: the scale that's always 2 kg heavy. A survey where people over-report voting because it's the “good citizen” answer.

Unreliability (random error)

Noise that goes up and down by chance: a wobbly scale, a confusing question people answer differently each time.

Frame 5.4

Three ways to judge validity

Face validity
On its face, does it look like it measures the concept?
Content validity
Does it cover all the concept's parts?
Construct validity
Does it relate to other things the way theory predicts?

In class: the prof's examples

  • Social capital: trust, networks, participation. Measuring only club memberships misses trust.
  • Political tolerance: asking everyone about the same fixed groups (e.g. communists) can make people look tolerant who simply don't dislike that group. The least-liked approach asks about the group each person dislikes most, because tolerance only means something toward people you oppose.
  • Human-rights event counts: places with a freer press or more NGOs report more abuses, so “more events” may mean “more reporting”, which is a bias.
  • Dong & Zhang's proxy: historical social capital measured with overseas students. Ask of any proxy: does it fit the purpose? the time and place? which dimensions does it leave out? what bias could enter?

The Great Voting Question · part 5

X, economic performance: KW use growth in real GDP per person in the election year. Other choices: unemployment, inflation, or how voters feel about the economy, and objective and subjective measures can disagree. Y: the incumbent party's share of the presidential vote. The loyalty camp measured party identification with one question (“Generally speaking, do you usually think of yourself as a Republican, a Democrat, an Independent, or what?”), turned into a 7-point scale. It was very reliable, but critics asked whether it is valid as a lifelong identity or partly a running tally of how parties have performed.

Words to keep

Concept
The idea you care about.
Operationalization
Turning the idea into a measurable procedure.
Reliability
Consistency.
Validity
Measuring the intended thing.
Bias
Error in one direction.
Proxy
A stand-in measure for something hard to observe.
DrumbeatReliable means consistent; valid means it measures the right idea. A measure can be reliable and still wrong.

Story 6 of 9 · Stage 2

KW Chapter 6 · Getting to know your data

Counting the Village

Two questions about any group: where is the middle, and how spread out are people around it?

The story

A visitor asks about your village: “How old are people here?” You can't recite every age. You'd say something like “most are around 30, but there are lots of children and a few very old grandparents.”

Without noticing, you've given the two things statistics always wants: a typical value (around 30) and how spread out people are (children to grandparents). This story gives those two ideas their proper names.

Frame 6.1

First, what kind of variable is it?

Categorical (nominal)
Names with no order: religion, party, country. Use the mode and percentages.
Ordinal
Ordered, but the gaps aren't equal: strongly agree / agree / disagree. Use the median.
Quantitative
Real amounts: age, income, vote share. The mean and SD make sense.

Never average category codes. If 1 = Catholic, 2 = Muslim, 3 = Protestant, a “mean religion of 2.1” means nothing. The numbers are just labels.

Frame 6.2

Where is the middle?

Mean
Add everything up, divide by how many. The balance point.
Median
Line everyone up; take the one in the middle. Half below, half above.
Mode
The most common value.

When a few extreme values pull to one side (skew), the mean gets dragged toward them, but the median hardly moves. That's why reports on incomes use the median.

Pause and think: {5, 5, 5} and {0, 5, 10}. Same mean?

Yes, both have mean 5. But the groups look completely different. The mean alone can't tell them apart, so we also need a measure of spread.

Frame 6.3

How spread out? The standard deviation (SD), in words first

The SD is roughly the typical distance between a person's value and the mean. Small SD: people are bunched near the middle. Big SD: they're spread far apart.

Let's build it with the prof's example: five districts with values 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 (the whole population).

  1. Find the mean. 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10 = 30. 30 ÷ 5 = 6.
  2. How far is each value from the mean? −4, −2, 0, +2, +4. (These always add to zero: the minuses cancel the pluses.)
  3. Square each distance, so nothing cancels and big distances count more: 16, 4, 0, 4, 16.
  4. Add them up: 40.
  5. Average them: 40 ÷ 5 = 8. This is the variance. Its units are “squared”, which is awkward.
  6. Take the square root to get back to normal units: √8 ≈ 2.83. That's the SD.

In one sentence: “The SD tells me how far, typically, values sit from the mean, in the variable's own units.”

Pause and think: why divide by n − 1 for a sample?

For a whole population (like the 5 districts) divide by N. For a sample, divide by n − 1 (40 ÷ 4 = 10, SD ≈ 3.16), because we used the sample's own mean, which uses up one piece of information and would otherwise make the spread look too small.

The Great Voting Question · part 6

Before testing anything, KW look at each variable: what's the typical incumbent vote share, how spread out are the 36 elections, are there odd elections far from the rest? Then they draw a scatterplot: one dot per election, growth across, vote share up. Getting to know your data comes before any test.

When you're ready: the symbols
μ = Σx ÷ NPopulation mean (“mu”): add all values (Σ means “add up”), divide by how many.
σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ NPopulation variance (“sigma squared”).
σ = √σ²Population SD.
s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)]Sample SD; x̄ (“x-bar”) is the sample mean.

Words to keep

Mean, median, mode
Balance point, middle person, most common.
Skew
A long tail on one side that drags the mean.
Variance
Average squared distance from the mean.
Standard deviation
Square root of the variance: typical distance from the mean.
DrumbeatFind the middle, measure the spread: distances → square → add → average → square root.

Story 7 of 9 · Stage 2

KW Chapter 7 · Probability and statistical inference

Tasting the Soup

Stir well, taste one spoon, judge the whole pot, and say how sure you are.

The story

You're cooking a big pot of soup. To check the salt, you don't drink the whole pot. You stir it well and taste one spoonful.

That spoonful tells you about the whole pot, but only if you stirred. If you skim only the top, where the oil floats, you'll get the wrong idea, and tasting a bigger spoonful from the top won't fix it.

And one spoonful might be a little saltier or blander than the pot on average, just by chance. Statistics can tell you how far off one spoonful is likely to be.

Frame 7.1

Pot and spoon: population and sample

Population
Everyone you want to know about (all Canadian voters).
Sampling frame
The actual list you pick from (e.g. a phone list). Gaps between list and population = coverage error.
Simple random sample
Everyone on the list has an equal chance of being picked: the stirring.
Parameter
The true number for the whole population (usually unknown): μ, σ.
Statistic
The number computed from the sample: x̄, s.
Sampling error
The gap between statistic and parameter that comes just from drawing one random sample.
Pause and think: can a bigger sample fix a badly chosen sample?

No. A bigger spoon from the oily top is still oily. Nonresponse, coverage gaps and convenience samples make you precisely wrong. In 1936 the Literary Digest poll got over 2 million responses and still called the US election wrong.

Frame 7.2

A little probability

A coin: P(heads) = .5. A bag with 3 red and 7 blue beads: P(red) = 3 ÷ 10 = .3.

  • Independent events don't affect each other. Two coin flips both heads: .5 × .5 = .25.
  • With replacement (put the bead back): P(red) stays .3 each time, so two reds = .3 × .3 = .09.
  • Without replacement: the second draw changes: 3/10 × 2/9 ≈ .067. The draws aren't independent.

Frame 7.3

The bell curve and “where do I stand?”

Many things pile up in a bell shape (the normal distribution). On any bell curve: about 68% of cases sit within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 (exactly 1.96), and 99.7% within 3.

A z-score says how many SDs a value is from the mean: z = (value − mean) ÷ SD.

Pause and think: test scores have mean 50 and SD 20. You scored 80. What's your z?

(80 − 50) ÷ 20 = 1.5. You're 1.5 SDs above average, higher than about 93% of people.

Frame 7.4

The big idea: SD vs SE

Imagine tasting many spoonfuls (many samples of the same size) and writing down each spoon's average saltiness. Those averages cluster much more tightly than single drops do. That's the sampling distribution, and it is bell-shaped even when individuals aren't (the central limit theorem).

SD

How much individuals differ from each other.

SE (standard error)

How much a sample's average would wobble from sample to sample. SE = SD ÷ √n.

Bigger samples → smaller SE (more precise). But because of the square root, you need 4 times the sample to halve the SE. And the SD doesn't shrink: people don't become more alike because you asked more of them.

“The SD tells me how spread out people are; the SE tells me how precise my estimate of the average is.”

Frame 7.5 ★

The 95% confidence interval

Estimate ± 1.96 × SE. The prof's example: 387 of 900 people approve of the president.

  1. Estimate: 387 ÷ 900 = .43, or 43%.
  2. SE of a proportion: √(.43 × .57 ÷ 900) ≈ .0165, or 1.65 points.
  3. Margin: 1.96 × 1.65 ≈ 3.24 points.
  4. Interval: 43 ± 3.24 → 39.76% to 46.24%.

Say it right: “If we took many samples of 900 and built an interval this way each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true approval rate.” Not “95% chance the truth is in this one”.

The Great Voting Question · part 7

Our 36 elections are like one spoonful of all the elections that could happen under the same political process. So any number we compute from them (like the slope in Story 9) comes with an SE: how much it would wobble with a different set of elections. Later, the slope's 95% interval is 0.31 to 0.94: we're fairly sure the relationship is positive, less sure exactly how big.

How many SDs from the mean.
Sampling distribution
How a statistic would vary over many samples.
Standard error
The spread of that sampling distribution: SD ÷ √n.

Story 8 of 9 · Stage 2

KW Chapter 8 · Bivariate hypothesis testing

The Dry Toothbrush

Assume the claim is true, then ask: how surprising is what I see?

The story

A child says, “I brushed my teeth.” You walk into the bathroom and touch the toothbrush. It's bone dry.

Notice how you reasoned. You started by assuming the child's claim (“they brushed”). Then you asked: if that were true, how likely is a dry toothbrush? Very unlikely. So you doubt the claim.

That's exactly how a hypothesis test works. The “claim” we assume is the null hypothesis: that nothing is going on.

Frame 8.1

The logic in four steps

  1. H₀ (null): no relationship in the population. “Growth and vote share are unrelated.”
  2. H₁ (alternative): there is a relationship.
  3. Pretend H₀ is true. Even then, samples won't match perfectly; chance makes small differences. Ask: how often would chance alone produce a result at least as extreme as ours? That's the p-value.
  4. Compare with α = .05 (chosen before looking). If p < .05, the result would be rare under H₀ → reject H₀ (“statistically significant”). Otherwise → fail to reject.

A p-value is P(evidence this extreme | H₀ true), like P(dry brush | they brushed). It is not the probability that H₀ is true.

Frame 8.2

Two ways to be wrong

Really no effectReally an effect
We reject H₀Type I error: a false alarm (the smoke detector beeps at toast). Chance = α.Correct
We don't rejectCorrectType II error: a missed fire. More likely with small samples.
prof's example

Scared Straight

Reoffended: 12 of 28 in the program (43%) vs 5 of 30 without it (17%). p ≈ .043 < .05 → reject “equal rates”. The program group did worse.

prof's example

Jordan's free throws

Michael Jordan makes 7 of 7, Vickers 3 of 7. p ≈ .07 → fail to reject “equal skill”. Obviously they're not equal: 7 shots is just too little evidence. A Type II error, and proof that fail to reject ≠ H₀ is true.

Frame 8.3

Which test? It depends on the kind of variables

X and Y are…TestClass example
Both categoriesChi-square (χ²), using a table of countsGender × 2016 vote
X categories, Y a numberDifference of means (t-test)Majority vs minority governments → days in office
Both numbersCorrelation (r)Growth × incumbent vote share

Each test follows the same logic: how big is the difference we see, compared with what chance alone would produce?

Frame 8.4

Test 1 · Chi-square: gender and vote

In KW's 2016 table (2,440 people): Clinton got 47.2% of men (532 of 1,128) and 56.2% of women (737 of 1,312), a gap of about 9 points.

  1. If gender and vote were unrelated (H₀), each gender would split like the total. Expected count = row total × column total ÷ grand total. Men voting Clinton: 1,128 × 1,269 ÷ 2,440 ≈ 586.65. We observed 532.
  2. For each cell: (observed − expected)² ÷ expected. Add the four cells: χ² ≈ 19.73.
  3. A 2×2 table has df = (2 − 1)(2 − 1) = 1. 19.73 is far beyond the 3.84 cutoff → p < .001 → reject independence.

The χ² number isn't the size of the gap; the 9-point difference is. And it doesn't prove gender causes vote choice (party ID, education, age could matter).

Frame 8.5

Test 2 · Difference of means: do majority governments last longer?

Majority governments lasted 930.5 days on average (124 governments); minority governments 674.4 days (53). Gap: 256.1 days.

t = gap ÷ SE of the gap = 256.1 ÷ 74.39 ≈ 3.44, with df = 124 + 53 − 2 = 175. p ≈ .0007 → reject “equal average duration”.

Overlapping individual durations don't mean the averages are equal; the test is about the averages and how precisely we know them.

Frame 8.6

Test 3 · Correlation: growth and vote

r measures how tightly points cluster around a straight line, from −1 to +1. 0 = no straight-line pattern.

For the 36 elections: r ≈ .569 (a positive, moderately strong pattern). Test of H₀ “no correlation”: t ≈ 4.03, df = 34, p ≈ .0003 → reject.

This is gate 3 (covariation) finally checked for our big question. It is not gates 1, 2 or 4.

Frame 8.7

What “significant” does not mean

  • Not important: tiny effects can be significant with huge samples.
  • Not well measured (Story 5).
  • Not a representative sample (Story 7).
  • Not causal (Stories 3–4).

The Great Voting Question · part 8

H₀: growth and incumbent vote are unrelated. Observed r = .569; t(34) = 4.03; p ≈ .0003. If there were truly no relationship, a pattern this strong would appear by chance about 3 times in 10,000. We reject H₀. Gate 3 is passed. Gate 4 (a hidden Z) is still open, and we still don't know how many votes a point of growth is worth. That's Story 9.

Words to keep

H₀ / H₁
No relationship / some relationship.
p-value
Chance of a result this extreme if H₀ were true.
α
The cutoff chosen in advance, usually .05.
Type I / Type II
False alarm / missed effect.
Two-sided
A difference in either direction counts (the default).
χ², t, r
The three tests for categories, means, and two numbers.
DrumbeatAssume nothing is going on; if what I see would be very rare under that assumption, I reject it. Rare under H₀ is not the same as H₀ being false for sure.

Story 9 of 9 · Stage 3

KW Chapter 9 · Two-variable regression

The Line Through the Elections

Draw the one straight line that best fits the dots. It tells you how much Y moves when X moves by one.

The story

A taxi charges a starting fee, then a fixed amount for every kilometre. If you know the distance, you can predict the fare: Fare = $3.50 + $1.75 × km.

Real life is messier: traffic, tips, detours. Real fares scatter around that rule. But the rule is still the best simple summary.

Regression does the same for our elections. It finds the best straight “fare rule” linking growth to vote share, then tells us how far real elections scatter around it.

Frame 9.1

Dots and a line

The line uses KW's real estimates; the dots are illustrative, not the actual 36 elections.

Each dot is one election. The points don't sit on a line; they scatter above and below a line that represents the general pattern. That scatter is normal.

Intercept (α)
Where the line starts: predicted vote when growth = 0. KW: 51.45%. (Like the taxi's $3.50.)
Slope (β)
How much predicted Y changes when X goes up by 1. KW: 0.625 vote points per growth point. (Like $1.75 per km.)
Fitted value (Ŷ)
The line's prediction for a case.
Residual (û)
The miss: actual minus predicted. Dots above the line have positive residuals.
Pause and think: predict the incumbent vote when growth is 3%.

51.45 + 0.625 × 3 = 51.45 + 1.875 = 53.33%.

Frame 9.2

Which line is “best”? Ordinary least squares (OLS)

You could draw many lines through the dots. OLS chooses the line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible: square every miss, add them up, and pick the line with the smallest total.

Why square? The same reason as the SD in Story 6: so misses above and below don't cancel, and big misses count more. KW compare three candidate lines; the best had the smallest squared total (about 792.6). OLS checks every possible line, not just three.

Frame 9.3 ★

Three numbers, three different jobs (the prof's announcement)

r (correlation)
Direction and strength of the straight-line pattern. Close to +1 or −1 = dots hug an upward or downward line. Our elections: .569.
Slope
How much predicted Y changes when X goes up by one unit, in real units. Our elections: 0.625 vote points per growth point.
R²
How well the line fits: the share of the variation in Y the line accounts for in this sample. R² = .64 means 64%. Our elections: .32.

With one X and an intercept: R² = r². Check it: .569 × .569 ≈ .32. ✓

Look at the pictures: the left and middle panels have the same slope but different r and R², because the dots are tighter or looser. The right panel has a gentle slope but a high r. Steepness and tightness are different things.

Pause and think: R² = .32. Does growth “cause 32% of election results”?

No. R² describes how well the line fits these 36 elections. It's a description of fit, not a causal share. A strong correlation or a high R² does not by itself establish causation.

Frame 9.4

Reading one line of regression output

Notice t = 4.03 is the same as the correlation test in Story 8. With one X, testing “slope = 0” and testing “r = 0” are the same test.

Frame 9.5

The honest limits

  • Association, not cause. Growth wasn't randomly assigned; a Z (a war, a scandal, party loyalty) could affect both. Gate 4 remains.
  • Elections, not individual voters. The line describes elections, not how any one person decided.
  • A typical miss of about 4.8 points is big when elections are won by a few points.
  • X must vary. If growth were the same every year, there'd be no slope to find.

The Great Voting Question · the end of this journey

Across 36 US elections, each extra point of growth goes with about 0.63 more vote points for the incumbent party (SE 0.155; 95% CI 0.31 to 0.94). The line explains about a third of the variation (R² = .32 = r²), and a typical election misses the line by about 4.8 points. The elder's saying holds up as a tendency, not a law, and not yet a proven cause. The fire is still burning: the next question (beyond this course) is what happens when we hold the hidden Zs constant.

The end of the journey

The whole story, told once

Read this the night before. If you can retell it in your own words, you understand the course.

The Great Voting Question, start to finish

  1. The fire (Story 1). An old saying: good harvests keep the chief in power. We turn it into a testable claim: X = economic growth, Y = incumbent party's vote share. Hypothesis: more growth, more votes. Null: no relationship.
  2. The craft (Story 2). A theory needs a mechanism: voters reward good times and punish bad ones. We use time-series variation (36 US elections, 1876–2016). A rival story, party loyalty, is a possible Z.
  3. The gates (Story 3). To call it causal it must pass four gates: mechanism (yes), direction (mostly yes, since growth comes before the vote), covariation (to be checked), and no hidden Z (still open).
  4. The coin (Story 4). We can't randomly assign economies, so the design is observational. Experiments, where a coin decides, are the gold standard for gate 4; natural experiments, cutoffs and instruments are clever substitutes.
  5. Naming (Story 5). “Economic performance” must be measured: growth in real GDP per person. Measures should be reliable (consistent) and valid (the right idea), and free of bias.
  6. Counting (Story 6). Get to know the data: centre (mean, median, mode) and spread (SD: distances → square → add → average → square root).
  7. Tasting (Story 7). Our elections are one spoonful. Any number from them wobbles; the SE says by how much. Estimate ± 1.96 SE gives a 95% interval.
  8. The toothbrush (Story 8). Assume no relationship. Would r = .569 be surprising? Very: t = 4.03, p ≈ .0003. Reject the null. Gate 3 is passed.
  9. The line (Story 9). The best-fitting line: Vote = 51.45 + 0.625 × Growth. Each growth point goes with about 0.63 vote points (CI 0.31–0.94). R² = .32 = .569². A tendency, well supported, but not yet proven to be a cause.

Traps the exam loves

✗ “Correlation proves causation.” ✓ It only passes gate 3.

✗ “p = .03 means a 3% chance H₀ is true.” ✓ It's the chance of data this extreme if H₀ were true.

✗ “Fail to reject means no effect.” ✓ It means this test couldn't detect one (Jordan's 7 shots).

✗ “A bigger sample fixes bias.” ✓ It only shrinks random error.

✗ “SD and SE are the same.” ✓ SD = people; SE = the estimate.

✗ “95% chance the true value is in this interval.” ✓ 95% of intervals built this way would contain it.

✗ “Average the category codes.” ✓ Codes are labels.

✗ “R² = .32 means growth causes 32% of results.” ✓ It describes fit in this sample.

✗ “Reliable means valid.” ✓ A consistently wrong scale is reliable.

✗ “Random sampling makes it causal.” ✓ That's random assignment.
